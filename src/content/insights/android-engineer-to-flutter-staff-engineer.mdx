In 2017 I was writing native Android for hospital patient-registration systems. Today I set
technical direction for a mobile platform. The code got easier. Everything else did not.

This is what actually changed.

## As a mid-level engineer, I optimised for correctness

The job was: make it work, make it not crash, ship it. Success was measurable and local. A
bug was mine, a feature was mine, and the feedback loop was short enough to learn from.

This is a genuinely good place to be, and I think engineers leave it too eagerly. Most of
what I rely on now was learned by being accountable for small things repeatedly.

## As a senior engineer, I started optimising for the team

The shift was uncomfortable, because the highest-value work stopped being the work I found
most satisfying.

Building up the mobile team meant that the review I gave someone else mattered more than the
feature I wrote that day. Improving the development experience — shorter feedback, fewer
sharp edges — helped every engineer every day, which beats any single fix I could ship.

The hardest habit to break was fixing things myself. It is faster once and slower forever.

## As a staff engineer, I optimise for decisions

At staff level the expensive mistakes are architectural, and they have a nasty property:
they are cheap to make and slow to surface. A bad boundary drawn today costs nothing this
sprint and costs a quarter eighteen months from now.

So the work is mostly:

**Setting direction** — deciding where the platform is going, and making that legible enough
that other people can make consistent decisions without asking.

**Engineering effectiveness** — the compounding stuff. Tooling, standards, feedback loops.

**Being glue** — sitting between product, backend, and platform teams, where mobile
constraints are easiest to miss until they are expensive.

**Mentorship and sponsorship** — the difference matters. Mentorship is advice. Sponsorship is
putting someone's name on the work.

<Callout title="The uncomfortable part">
  The highest-leverage week I have often produces no commits. That was hard to accept, and I
  do not think I would have accepted it without having spent years being measured on output.
</Callout>

## What did not change

Depth. The reason a staff engineer's direction is worth following is that it is grounded in
having actually built the thing. I still write plugins, still debug platform-specific
crashes, still read the native source when the documentation is thin.

An architect who has not shipped recently is guessing, confidently.

## What I would tell my 2017 self

The seniority ladder is not a progression from writing code to attending meetings. It is a
progression in the *blast radius of your mistakes* — and the discipline required scales with
it.
